## Notes

Quarto! (designed by Blaise Müller, ~1985) is, in Goossens' framing, a
generalised 4×4 variation of tic-tac-toe with one twist: you do not choose
your own piece. Your opponent hands you the piece you must place, and then you
hand them theirs. This record covers the standard ruleset, where a winning
"quarto" is four in a line (row, column or one of the two diagonals) sharing
any one of the four binary attributes. The popular advanced variant, which
also counts four matching pieces in any 2×2 square, is a different game; its
value is not established in these sources.

Two solves exist, and both give a draw. In summer 1998 Luc Goossens solved the
game weakly by alpha-beta search with exhaustive symmetry reduction,
establishing the value from the start and that the deepest forced win is at
level 4+1. His original web writeup was later removed, and the result, though
widely cited (Wikipedia), had no independent corroboration until Goossens
re-solved it himself in 2026 with three completely independent C solvers: one
hand-coded, and two generated by Anthropic's Claude and Google's Gemini 3.1
Pro. All three reported identical results.

The 2026 paper uses the game's full symmetry group. Each board has up to
32·16·24 = 12,288 equivalents (32 board transforms, 16 attribute inversions,
24 attribute permutations), which brings the naive (16!)² ≈ 4.38×10^26 game
tree down to a feasible brute-force depth-first evaluation. All 148 unique 2+1
configurations are draws, which proves both players can force a draw. All 3382
unique 3+1 configurations are draws or trivial wins, so there are no
non-trivial wins above level 4+1. Of 91,746 unique 4+1 configurations, 2,959
are non-trivial wins (~3%).

These symmetry counts match Kerner and Angluin (2001) for 2+1 and 3+1. Their
4+1 count of 91,558 differs from Goossens' 91,746 by exactly the 188
configurations that already form a quarto with the four pieces placed.

In 2023 Cameron Cheung of GamesCrafters (UC Berkeley) solved Quarto strongly,
as a full tablebase with a value for every reachable position, using
tier-based retrograde analysis under Dan Garcia's GAMESMAN architecture. The
run took 3.51 days on a single 12-core CPU. Shi's 2024 EECS tech report
documents it and gives Quarto "over 11 quadrillion positions" (~1.1×10^16).
That count includes which piece is currently being handed over and is not
reduced by symmetry, so it is far larger than Goossens' per-level counts of
unique configurations (~10^3–10^5).
